    0.1

    What a 3D printer actually does

    Basics

    Our printers use FDM (fused deposition modeling). Picture a hot glue gun on a robot arm. A spool of plastic string called filament, 1.75 mm thick, is pushed into a nozzle heated to about 200–300 °C. The plastic melts, and the printer draws it onto a flat build plate one thin slice at a time. Each slice cools and hardens in seconds, and the next one is drawn on top of it.

    The numbers behind it:

    • The nozzle opening is 0.4 mm wide, so each line of plastic is about 0.42 mm wide.
    • Each slice, called a layer, is usually 0.2 mm tall. A 40 mm tall part is 200 layers.
    • The X1C moves the nozzle at up to 500 mm/s and still lays down hundreds of meters of line in one part. That's why prints take hours, not minutes.

    Two facts follow from "one layer at a time". Most of this course comes back to them:

    1. It can't print on air. Every layer needs something under it: the plate, the layer below, or temporary supports.
    2. Layers are fused, not solid. A part is strong along its layers and weaker between them, so the way you lay a part on the plate changes how strong it is.
    Why we print robot parts

    A printed bracket costs a few dollars of plastic and is ready overnight. A machined one takes days. Print it, test-fit it, fix it in CAD, print again. That fast loop is the reason the team owns six printers.

    0.2

    Layers, walls & infill

    Basics›What the slicer makes

    A slicer (ours is Bambu Studio) cuts your 3D model into layers and plans every move the nozzle makes on each one. Drag through the layers of this knob. It has a wide base, a thin stem, a cap that flares out, and a rounded top.

    • Green rings are the walls, the outline traced around each layer. They do most of the work.
    • Gold lines are infill, a light lattice inside. The part is mostly air, which saves hours and plastic.
    • The first few layers and the top layers are printed solid, so the part has a closed skin.
    • Red marks plastic printed on air, an overhang. Turn on supports to see the fix.

    The words you'll hear

    Basics›Vocabulary
    A printed part, cut in half
    Filament
    The plastic "string" on a spool, 1.75 mm thick. Comes in different materials (PLA, PETG, TPU, nylon…).
    Nozzle & hot end
    The heated metal tip that melts filament. Ours is a 0.4 mm hole.
    Build plate
    The flat, heated, removable sheet the part is printed on.
    Layer · layer height
    One horizontal slice of the part, and how tall it is (usually 0.2 mm).
    Wall (wall loop)
    The outline traced around each layer. More walls make a stronger part.
    Infill (sparse infill)
    The light lattice inside a part, given as a percentage (15% is mostly air).
    Top & bottom shells
    Solid layers that close off the top and bottom of the part.
    First layer
    The layer pressed onto the plate. If it doesn't stick, the print fails.
    Overhang
    A surface that leans out past the layer below it. Past about 45° it droops.
    Bridge
    A flat span printed across a gap between two supports, like a bridge deck.
    Support
    Temporary scaffolding under overhangs, snapped off after printing.
    Brim
    A thin flat ring around the bottom of a part that helps it stay stuck down.
    Slicer
    The software that turns a 3D model into layers and nozzle moves. Ours is Bambu Studio.
    Sliced file (G-code)
    The slicer's output: the exact instructions one printer model follows.

    Meet the X1C

    The machine itself: what can hurt you, what every part does, how to drive the touchscreen, and how our six printers are organized.

    1.1

    Safety on the X1C

    Printer

    The X1C is enclosed and has cameras and sensors watching the print, which makes it feel safe. It still has a nozzle that reaches 300 °C, a bed that reaches 120 °C on our US power, and a toolhead that moves at up to 500 mm/s. The enclosure and AI detection reduce risk. They don't replace you.

    • Hot zones: the nozzle and heater block, the build plate and heatbed, and the inside of the chamber after ABS or ASA prints. The touchscreen shows live temperatures, so check them before reaching in. Below 40 °C is safe to touch.
    • Moving parts: the toolhead moves in X and Y, and the bed moves in Z. Never reach in while it's printing. Tap Pause first and wait for the toolhead to park.
    • The filament cutter on the toolhead is a real blade. Keep fingers off the cutter lever on the right side of the toolhead.
    • Fumes: print ABS, ASA, PC and nylon with the door and lid closed. The X1C's chamber fan pulls air through an activated carbon filter. That cuts the smell, but it's not a fume hood, so the room still needs ventilation.
    • AI detection is a helper, not a babysitter. Spaghetti detection and first-layer inspection catch many failures, not all of them. Follow the team's unattended-printing rule.
    • Safety glasses when cutting supports or brims with flush cutters.
    Emergency

    Smoke, a burning smell, or a blob of plastic on the hot end: tap Stop on the touchscreen, then turn off the power switch on the back of the printer. Get a mentor. Don't open the door into smoke and don't pull plastic off the hot end. Know where the fire extinguisher is before your first print.

    Pause vs Stop

    Pause parks the toolhead and keeps temperatures, so you can resume. Stop cancels the job for good, and an X1C print can't be resumed after a stop. Use Pause to check something. Use Stop when the print is ruined or unsafe.

      1.4

      Our fleet of six

      Shop

      We run six identical X1Cs, each with one standard 4-slot AMS and its own microSD card. The hardware is identical, so one Bambu Studio printer preset works for all six. What differs is what's loaded in each AMS, so always check the printer you are about to use.

      Example AMS loads for illustration. Your lead decides what lives in each printer.

      • Printers are numbered X1C-1 to X1C-6 with a label on the front frame. Each printer's microSD card is labeled with the same number and stays with that printer.
      • AMS slots are A1 to A4: AMS "A", slots 1 through 4, left to right. The screen and Bambu Studio both use these names.
      • Pick your printer before you slice. You need its plate type and the filament in its AMS so your file matches.
      • Log every print in the team print log: date, your name, printer number, part, material and AMS slot, plate, estimated time and grams, and the result.
      • Engineering materials have homes. If the lead assigns a printer to CF or nylon (dried spool on the external holder, engineering plate), keep PLA jobs off it when the robot needs parts.

      2.2

      Build plates

      Bambu Studio›Plate type+Printer

      The X1C uses magnetic, flexible steel plates with different surfaces. The plate type you pick in Bambu Studio sets the bed temperature, and the X1C checks the plate it sees. The plate in Studio must match the plate in the printer.

      Plate picker · choose a material
      PLA + hot plate + closed door = clog

      On a textured or smooth PEI plate, PLA's bed runs about 55–65 °C. With the door and lid shut, the chamber warms up, PLA softens above the nozzle, and the hot end jams. This is called heat creep. Bambu's guidance: for PLA with the bed above 45 °C, open the front door and lift or remove the top glass if the AMS allows. With the AMS on top we can't remove the lid, so open the door. The Cool Plate (35 °C) avoids the problem.

      Plate care

      • Hold plates by the edges. Skin oil is the number one cause of prints not sticking.
      • Wipe with 90%+ isopropyl alcohol before prints. Once a week (or when parts stop sticking), wash with dish soap and hot water, rinse and dry.
      • Glue stick goes on in a thin, even layer, and only where the part will sit. It's a release layer on smooth PEI as much as a grip layer.
      • Never scrape the PEI with a metal tool. Flex the plate instead.
      2.3

      The AMS

      Printer›AMS+Bambu Studio › Filament

      Each printer has one standard AMS (Automatic Material System) on top: a sealed box holding four spools in slots A1 to A4. It feeds the chosen spool through PTFE tubes to the toolhead. When a print needs a different slot, the toolhead cuts the filament, the AMS pulls it back, and the new one feeds in. The X1C flushes the old color into the purge chute.

      Loading a spool

      1. Open the AMS lid. Put the spool on the rollers so it unwinds from the bottom toward the back, the way the slot's arrow shows.
      2. Cut the filament end at a sharp angle and straighten the last 10 cm.
      3. Push the end into the slot's feeder until the AMS grabs it and pulls it in. Let go once it grips.
      4. Bambu spools have an RFID tag, so the AMS reads the type and color by itself. Other brands: tap the slot on the touchscreen (or in Studio's Device tab) and set it to the matching Generic type (Generic PLA, Generic PETG…) and the spool's color.
      5. Close the lid. The AMS is sealed with desiccant to keep filament dry, so don't leave it open.

      Using it from Bambu Studio

      • In the Filament panel, click Sync (the AMS sync button) to pull the target printer's AMS contents into your project. Now your filament list matches what's actually loaded.
      • When you print, Studio (or the touchscreen) asks which slot each filament in the file should come from. This is filament mapping. Map PETG to the PETG slot. The temperatures are baked into the file, so a wrong map prints the wrong plastic at the wrong temperature.
      • Auto-refill: if two slots hold the same filament (same type and color), the AMS can switch to the backup when one runs out.
      Team rule: non-Bambu filament = Generic preset

      For any other brand, pick the matching Generic preset in Studio, for example Generic PETG @BBL X1C or Generic PLA @BBL X1C, and set the AMS slot to the same Generic type. Don't build custom filament presets. The Generic ones are tuned by Bambu and keep all six printers consistent. If a Generic print strings or under-extrudes, check that the spool is dry before blaming the preset.

      What not to put in the AMS

      • Soft TPU (95A and softer) jams in the tubes. Use the external spool holder on the back and the Ext slot.
      • Third-party carbon or glass-fiber filament is often brittle, snaps in the tubes and wears them out. Use the external spool.
      • Wet or brittle filament (old PLA that snaps when bent) breaks inside the AMS and is painful to clear.
      • Cardboard spools slip and shed dust. Put them on a spool adapter ring or respool them first.
      External spool (Ext)

      Hang the spool on the holder at the back, set Ext on the touchscreen to the right material, then tap Load. Push the filament into the toolhead's inlet until the extruder grabs it. In Studio's print dialog, turn off Use AMS for that job.

      Never yank

      Never pull filament out of the AMS or the toolhead by hand while it's loaded. Use Unload so the cutter and AMS do it properly. A yanked filament can leave a broken piece in the tubes or hub that a mentor then has to dig out.

      Step by step

      1. Switch the workspace toolbar to the UTILITIES tab.
      2. In the MAKE panel, click 3D Print.
      3. Selection: click the body (or component) you want to print. Export one part per file unless you're batching identical parts.
      4. Format: 3MF. (Fusion also offers STL. Use 3MF.)
      5. Refinement: High, or Custom with a small surface deviation. Low refinement turns round holes into polygons, and bearings and hex shafts won't fit.
      6. Uncheck Send to 3D print utility. Then OK asks you where to save the file. Leave it checked only if a lead has set Fusion to hand off to Bambu Studio directly.
      7. Save it with the team file name (below) into the team's print folder, then open it in Bambu Studio with File › Import or by dragging it onto the plate.

      Fusion moves things around between updates. If the dialog looks slightly different, find the same four choices: body, 3MF, refinement, and send to utility (off).

      Team file names

      Use Subsystem-Part_Material_vRev, for example Intake-Bracket_PETG_v3.3mf. The name carries through to the sliced file and the printer screen, so you can find your print among six printers' worth of files.

      Before you export

      Check that the part is a single solid body with no zero-thickness faces, that holes have the clearances from Unit 5, and that the face that will sit on the plate is flat. Fixing these in Fusion takes a minute. Fixing them after a 6-hour print costs 6 hours.

      3.6

      Per-object settings & modifiers

      Bambu Studio›Objects list›Right-click

      Global settings apply to everything on the plate. Sometimes one part, or one region of a part, needs something different. Studio handles this without a second slice.

      • Per-object settings: select the object, then switch the Process panel from Global to Objects. Changes now apply to that object only. You can print a PLA test bracket at 2 walls next to a PETG part at 5 walls.
      • Modifier: right-click the object and choose Add modifier. Pick a box, cylinder or sphere, then move and scale it over the area that needs reinforcing. Give the modifier its own settings, for example 100% infill and 6 walls around a bolt boss, while the rest stays light.
      • Height range modifier: applies settings to a band of Z heights. Useful for a thin neck that needs more walls.
      • Negative part: subtracts a shape from the object. It's a quick hole without going back to Fusion, but fix the CAD for anything permanent.
      Classic robot use

      A bracket bolted to the drivetrain: 4 walls and 25% gyroid for the body, plus a cylinder modifier around each bolt hole at 100% infill. Strong where the bolts clamp, light everywhere else, and the slice is barely slower.

      3.8

      Read the Preview before you print

      Bambu Studio›Slice plate›Preview

      Click Slice plate and Studio switches to Preview. Two minutes here saves hours of failed printing.

      • Vertical slider (right): drag down through the layers. Look at the first layer's contact area, where supports start, and the layers around holes and overhangs.
      • Horizontal slider (bottom): plays the toolpath inside one layer. Use it to see the order walls print in.
      • Legend, Line Type view: outer wall, inner wall, sparse infill, internal solid infill, top surface, support, and so on. Walls should form a thick band on structural parts.
      • Other views: Speed, Layer time (very short layers on tiny parts can melt), Flow, and Filament (which AMS slot prints what).
      • Estimates panel: total time and grams of filament per slot. Check that the spool in that slot has more than that left.
      Red flags in Preview

      A thin wall that shows only one line or disappears. Supports landing on a surface that needs to be smooth. Floating islands in mid-air with no support under them. A first layer that touches the plate with only a tiny area on a tall part. Any warning in the top-right message area. Fix these first, then re-slice.

      4.1

      The microSD card workflow

      Bambu Studio›Print plate ▾›Export plate sliced file→Printer › Print Files

      This is how we normally print. Studio exports the sliced plate as a .gcode.3mf file: G-code plus a thumbnail, filament list and plate info, which the X1C screen can read. You carry it to the printer on that printer's microSD card. Step through all nine steps.

      microSD walkthrough

      SD card rules for six printers

      • Each card belongs to its printer and is labeled X1C-1 to X1C-6. Return it to the same printer.
      • Save to the root of the card, not inside the folders already on it. The X1C uses those for its own data (timelapses, cache).
      • Export one plate per file with Export plate sliced file. Avoid the plain Export G-code option. A plain .gcode file has no thumbnail or filament info, so the screen can't show a preview or map AMS slots properly.
      • Eject the card in your computer before pulling it. Pulling it mid-write corrupts the file.
      • Never remove the card while that printer is printing from it. The printer reads the file as it goes.
      • Clean up your old files once your part is done. A full card makes the next person's export fail.
      • If a card won't show up on the printer or a computer, don't format it yourself. Give it to a lead.

      Removal & cleanup

      Printer
      1. Wait until the bed reads below about 35 °C. Most parts release on their own as the plate cools.
      2. Lift the plate off its magnet and pins, and flex it to pop the part off. Don't pry with a scraper unless a lead shows you how.
      3. Peel glue residue off with a wet cloth. Wipe the plate with IPA and put it back so it sits flat against the pins at the back.

      Designing robot parts

      How a printed part actually carries load, and the Fusion habits that make parts fit real hardware on the first try.

      5.1

      Orientation is strength

      Bambu Studio›Rotate / Lay on face

      A print is a stack of layers fused while hot. Along a layer, the plastic is one continuous strand. Between layers, the bond can be half as strong or less. Turn the part so the load runs along the layers.

      L-bracket under load

      1. Find the load: where is the part pulled, bent, twisted or hit?
      2. Lay it so the load runs along the layers. Use Lay on face (F) in Studio. Don't trust Auto orient for structural parts.
      3. Then deal with supports, or split the part with Cut (C). Strength wins over a cleaner underside.
      4. Standoffs and pegs that stick up from the plate snap at the base. Print them lying down, or run a bolt through them.
      5.2

      Design rules in Fusion

      Fusion›Design
      ≤ 45° from vertical

      Overhangs

      Each layer can stick out about halfway past the one below. Steeper needs support. Short bridges (≤ 10 mm) print fine on the X1C with good cooling.

      45° chamfer on the bottom

      Bottom edges

      A fillet touching the plate is an overhang. A 0.5–1 mm chamfer prints cleanly and hides elephant's foot.

      ≥ 4 × 0.42 mm walls

      Wall thickness

      Structural walls of at least 2–3 mm let Studio fit 4–6 wall loops. Avoid walls under 0.8 mm.

      +0.15–0.25 mm per side

      Clearance fits

      Holes print a little small. Start with 0.2 mm per side for slip fits on our X1Cs and 0–0.1 mm for press fits. Confirm with a test gauge.

      Teardrop top

      Horizontal holes

      A round hole on its side sags at the top. Make the top a 45° point (teardrop) so it needs no support.

      Heat-set inserts

      Threads you'll reuse

      Don't tap plastic. Model the insert's hole size and press brass inserts in with the insert tip on the soldering iron. Or design hex pockets for #10-32 or 1/4-20 nyloc nuts.

      1–3 mm fillet

      Inside corners

      Sharp inside corners crack. Fillet them in the plane of the layers.

      ≤ 256 mm

      Build volume

      The X1C prints up to 256 mm in every direction. Bigger parts get split in Fusion with alignment pins or bolts across the joint.

      ≥ 0.6 mm

      Labels

      Emboss or deboss the part number at least 0.6 mm, on a top or side face, in a bold sans font.

      Hex shafts and bearings

      Print a stepped test gauge in the material you'll use (for example a 1/2" hex in steps of +0.05 mm), test real shafts and bearings, and write the winning size on the shop's fit card. Studio's X-Y hole compensation (Quality tab, Advanced) can nudge every hole on a part, but fix repeat problems in Fusion so the next person gets them right too.

      5.3

      Supports & hard shapes

      Bambu Studio›Process › Support+Support painting

      Supports are throwaway scaffolding printed under anything that would otherwise be printed on air. They cost plastic and time, leave rough marks, and have to be cut off. Good operators use them on purpose, not by default.

      When a part needs them

      • Steep overhangs: surfaces leaning out more than about 45° from vertical. Studio's Threshold angle is measured from horizontal, and its default is 30°.
      • Islands: a feature that starts in mid-air, like the underside of a T or a tab that sticks out halfway up.
      • Long bridges: flat spans longer than about 10–15 mm.

      Avoid them first: reorient the part, ask the designer for a 45° chamfer under a ledge, use teardrop holes, or split the part with Cut. Strength still comes first (5.1).

      Choosing where supports go: Support painting

      1. Select the part and open Support painting in the toolbar above the plate.
      2. Paint blockers where supports must never go: inside bolt holes, hex bores and bearing seats. Supports in there are nearly impossible to clean out.
      3. Paint enforcers under small features the automatic supports missed.
      4. Automatic types (tree(auto), normal(auto)) add supports wherever needed but obey your blockers. Manual types (tree(manual), normal(manual)) add supports only where you painted enforcers.
      5. Turn on On build plate only when you can, so supports stand on the plate instead of scarring the part.
      6. Re-slice and check Preview with the support line type showing. Count the grams. Supports can double a print's plastic.

      Getting supports off cleanly

      • Let the part cool completely. Warm supports bend instead of snapping.
      • Safety glasses on. Grip with pliers, twist, and pull away from the part. Trim what's left with flush cutters, cutting away from your hand.
      • Clean the contact marks with a deburring tool or a file.
      • If the undersides keep coming out ugly, raise Top Z distance a little (for example 0.2 → 0.25 mm) so supports release more easily.

      5.4

      Finishing: heat-set inserts, holes & cleanup

      Shop bench

      Installing a heat-set insert

      1. Check the part first. The hole size comes from the insert maker's chart, and it's smaller than the insert. The hole should be about 1 mm deeper than the insert. In Studio, the area around it needs at least 4 wall loops or a 100% infill modifier (3.6).
      2. Fit the insert tip on the soldering iron. Start the iron near the filament's print temperature: about 220 °C for PLA, 240–250 °C for PETG, higher for PAHT-CF.
      3. Hold or clamp the part so the hole faces straight up. Safety glasses on.
      4. Set the insert on the hole, small end down, and put the tip into it.
      5. Press gently and straight down and let the heat do the work. It should sink in 5–10 seconds. If it won't move with light pressure, raise the iron 10 °C. Forcing it melts a crooked, oversized hole.
      6. Stop when it's flush or just below the surface, then lift the iron straight out.
      7. While the plastic is still soft, press the insert square with a flat metal block. Let it cool for a minute.
      8. Test it by threading the bolt in by hand. It should go in straight without spinning the insert.
      Iron safety

      The tip is as hot as a nozzle. Always park it in its stand, never on the bench, and switch it off when you walk away.

      Getting holes to exact size

      • Bolt holes: run a drill bit of the exact size through by hand or at low speed. High speed melts plastic onto the bit.
      • Bearing seats: ream or sand a little at a time, test-fitting often. The better fix is the right size in CAD, from your fit card (Lab 1).
      • Hex bores: clean the corners with a small file. Never hammer a shaft through, because it splits the layers apart.

      Cleanup

      • Trim brims with flush cutters and clean edges with a deburring tool.
      • Sand mating faces flat if needed. Don't heat-gun near thin features.
      • Write the part number on it with a paint marker if it isn't embossed.

      Troubleshooting & care

      Read the X1C's error messages, recognize common failures, and keep six printers healthy through build season.

      6.1

      HMS alerts: the X1C telling you something

      Printer+Bambu Studio › Device

      The X1C's HMS (Health Management System) raises an alert when something's off: AMS feed failures, a nozzle temperature fault, a detected spaghetti print, and so on. Each alert has a code and a short message. Some pause the print, and some are just warnings.

      1. Read the message fully on the screen. Don't just tap it away.
      2. Write the code down in the print log. Repeat codes on one printer show a lead which machine needs attention.
      3. Look it up. In Studio's Device tab or Bambu Handy, the alert links to the Bambu wiki page for that exact code, with the cause and fix steps.
      4. Fix what you're allowed to fix (reload filament, clear the purge chute, clean the plate), then resume. Hardware or temperature faults go to a lead.

      6.3

      Maintenance for six X1Cs

      Printer›Bambu wiki · X1 maintenance

      Every print

      • Plate wiped with IPA
      • Nozzle tip free of old plastic
      • Purge bin checked
      • AMS lid closed afterwards

      Weekly

      • Wash PEI plates with soap and water
      • Clean the nozzle wiper and chute area
      • Wipe the carbon rods with IPA on a lint-free cloth
      • Check AMS humidity and desiccant

      Monthly · lead

      • Inspect the carbon rods. Clean more often when printing ABS or ASA.
      • Lubricate the Z lead screws and other steel rods per the wiki
      • Check PTFE tubes and the AMS hub for wear
      • Check belt tension per the wiki procedure

      As needed · lead

      • Replace the carbon filter (about every 3 months if printing ABS or ASA often)
      • Hot end swap: replace the whole hot end, then set nozzle type and size on the printer and in Studio
      • Firmware updates, one printer at a time, with a test print
      • Full calibration after the printer is moved
      Never oil the carbon rods

      The X1C's X-axis carbon rods run on self-lubricating bearings. Bambu says grease or oil on them causes resistance and makes them harder to clean. Clean them with IPA only. Lubrication is for the Z lead screws and steel rods, on the schedule in Bambu's wiki.

      Official reference: Bambu Lab wiki, X1 basic maintenance. Leads should follow it for anything in the Monthly and As-needed columns.
